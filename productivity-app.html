⚠️ Why this can't count steps while the app is closed

This is a limitation of the web platform, not a setting that can be switched on. When a browser tab is closed or the screen locks, the browser stops delivering motion sensor events entirely and suspends the page's JavaScript. There is no web API — none, on any browser — that lets a website read the accelerometer in the background. Service workers can wake for network events and notifications, but they have no sensor access at all.

Your phone's built-in health app does this because it isn't a web page: it's native code with permission to run against a dedicated low-power motion coprocessor that counts steps in hardware, all day, without waking the main processor. A website cannot reach that chip.

What that means in practice: automatic counting here works while this screen is open and awake — a deliberate walk with the app in front of you. It will always under-count your true daily total, because it cannot see the walking you do the rest of the time.

The accurate options, in order:
1. Read the number from Apple Health or Google Fit at the end of the day and enter it below. That number comes from the motion coprocessor and is the most accurate figure available to you.
2. Wrap this app with Capacitor or a similar native shell, which can request HealthKit / Health Connect permission and read all-day counts properly. That's a build step, not a change inside this file.
3. Use the automatic tracking here for individual walks, where it's genuinely useful, and treat the daily total as a floor rather than a full picture.

🎯 Calibrate the counter

If the automatic count is consistently off on your phone, correct it here. Walk a counted number of steps with tracking on, then enter both figures — the app works out the correction factor and applies it to future counts. This compensates for how you carry your phone and how you walk, which are the two largest sources of error.
Steps are only counted when you're actually walking. Every few hundred milliseconds the app checks whether your phone's motion is genuinely rhythmic (an autocorrelation test across the human gait band), and holds detected steps in a buffer until eight consistent ones confirm a real walking bout. Scrolling, typing, fidgeting, gesturing while talking, and car or train journeys all fail that test and are discarded rather than counted.

It also can't read an Apple Watch or Wear OS watch — smartwatches don't run web pages, that needs a native watch app.

Your phone's own OS tracks this properly across every app — far more accurately than any website could:

🩺 General guidance

There's no single official medical number for adult screen time the way there sometimes is for children — expert bodies mostly avoid a strict hour limit and focus on how screens are used. Commonly cited, reasonable benchmarks:
• Roughly 2–4 hours/day of recreational (non-work) screen use is the range generally considered reasonable for adults. • The "20-20-20" rule for eye strain: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. • Avoid screens for the last 30–60 minutes before bed — blue light and stimulation can delay sleep onset. • For children, the WHO and AAP publish age-specific guidance (generally far stricter than adult benchmarks, especially under age 5) — worth checking their sites directly if this app is used by a child in your household.

🕵️ Is something (like a VPN) slowing you down?

Honest answer: a website has no way to detect whether a VPN or any other app is running on your device — that's invisible to browser JavaScript by design, the same privacy boundary covered elsewhere in this app. But there's a genuinely reliable way to find out yourself: run the speed test above with your VPN on, then again with it off, and compare the numbers. Whatever difference you see is the real effect of that VPN on your connection — no guessing needed.
If this location doesn't match where you actually are, that's a real, visible sign your traffic is being routed elsewhere (VPN or proxy) — check it with your VPN on vs. off for the clearest picture.

📄 SAR value — why this can't be "measured" live

Being upfront: SAR (Specific Absorption Rate) is not something any app can read in real time, on this or any device. It's a fixed laboratory value — measured once by the manufacturer under standardized test conditions when a specific phone model is certified, then published as a static regulatory disclosure. There's no sensor for it, no live "SAR reading" exists, and any app claiming to show one in real time is displaying a made-up number.
To find the real, official SAR value for your exact phone model:
iPhone: Settings → General → Legal & Regulatory → look for the SAR/RF exposure section, or search "[your iPhone model] SAR value" on apple.com.
Android: Usually in Settings → About phone → Regulatory labels, or dial *#07# on many models, or check the manufacturer's site.
Any phone (most authoritative): find your phone's FCC ID (Settings → About, or printed under the battery/SIM tray on older phones), then look it up at fcc.gov/oet/ea/fccid for the official test report.

Reminders are off.
What to expect, honestly. Reminders fire reliably while the app is open, including in a background tab. Once the app is fully closed, delivery depends on your browser: Chrome on Android can wake the app periodically to check (roughly hourly at best, and not at all on battery saver), so a reminder may arrive late. iOS Safari won't wake it at all. Anything missed while closed is shown here the next time you open the app.

Exact-to-the-minute delivery while fully closed needs a push server, which this app deliberately doesn't have — it's a single file with no backend and no account. For appointments you can't miss, keep using your phone's own calendar alarm.
